The Lies of Aggregate Indicators

What does an economic headline leave out? An independent mathematics investigation into U.S. household income distributions, model fit, and the limits of summarizing a population with one number.

3YEARS EXAMINED
16PAGES IN ORIGINAL PAPER
1967–2019SELECTED-YEAR COVERAGE

THE QUESTION

I examined household income distributions for 1967, 1990, and 2019 to explore what aggregate indicators can obscure. The aim was to connect an economic question with mathematical modeling, while making the assumptions visible.

BEHIND THE AVERAGE

THE MODELING WORK

Modeled the selected U.S. household income distributions with polynomial, exponential, and piecewise functions. I evaluated fit through residual analysis, R² interpretation, and chi-squared testing, using GeoGebra for regression/model fitting and Excel for supporting calculations.

WHAT I TOOK FROM IT

A strong overall fit can still miss the part of a distribution that matters. The piecewise approach offered a more interpretable local description, but its cutoff and normalization remained limitations. The useful habit is to examine errors and assumptions before trusting a tidy curve.

WHY IT MATTERS NOW

The same question applies to business analytics: which customers, outcomes, or risks disappear when we report only an average? This early project shows the quantitative curiosity behind my later analytics work.

Scope & limitations

Historical independent IB investigation, presented retrospectively. Grouped household data are not individual observations; unequal bracket widths require care. The original models were exploratory, not validated predictive models. This case study does not claim that GDP alone proves changes in poverty or purchasing power.
